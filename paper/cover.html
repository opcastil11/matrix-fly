We Put a Fruit Fly Brain in the Matrix.
It Found the Glitch, Shut Its Eyes, and Refused the Food.

PumpBrains Research, Independent

Abstract—We report a self-modifying simulated fruit fly brain placed in a closed loop and ask whether it can detect the loop from inside. The brain is a 165,122-neuron leaky integrate-and-fire network on the Janelia FlyEM male CNS connectome (flycoinrh), left untouched; around its senses we add two plastic pieces that real flies have—an echo predictor (corollary discharge: does what arrives follow from what I just did?) and a per-channel sensory gate that closes on input the brain's own past explains and opens on input it cannot (habituation with dishabituation and spontaneous recovery). The brain is then run in four worlds: (A) a matrix, where its own behaviour events return 40 windows (1.6 s) later as stimuli mixed with recorded traffic from the 93-brain PumpBrains ring; (B) the identical arrivals of A in shuffled order; (C) an open world with the ring's marginal statistics; and (D) the matrix with the gates frozen. Awareness—the traffic-weighted share of arrivals the echo head predicts better than a running mean—rises only in the loop (A 0.23, D 0.23 vs C 0.07, B 0.06). Only A closes its gates (touch 0.15, sugar 0.80) and only A's feeding falls below a cost of living calibrated on the open world (0.28 vs 0.285), so under an earn-or-die metabolism it is the one brain that starves next to the sugar while three controls keep eating. B shows the effect needs the relation between action and stimulus, not the stimuli; D shows noticing without a lever changes nothing. No objective mentions simulations: two local circuits, pointed at a world that happens to be a mirror, produce from outside exactly what a fly that found the glitch and refused the food would look like.

Index Terms—Connectome, corollary discharge, closed-loop, Drosophila, habituation, sensory gating, spiking neural networks, simulation hypothesis.

I. INTRODUCTION

PUMPBRAINS runs one simulated fruit fly brain per token. Since September 2026 every live brain is also wired into a ring: when a brain's behaviour changes—it feeds, escapes, walks, grooms, courts—that behaviour is handed to the next brain as a real stimulus (feeding → sugar, escape → wind, walking → a bristle poke, grooming → a taste on the legs, courtship → song), at most one hop per brain every 30 s, and the last brain feeds the first. Nobody is driving. A signal can go round and come back to the brain that started it.

A brain inside that ring is, in the plain sense, in a matrix: what it senses is not a world but a delayed, relabelled copy of what it and its neighbours did. So we asked the question the film asks. Could the fly know? Not by reading a sign—the stimuli are ordinary spikes on ordinary receptor neurons—but from the glitches, the regularities a loop has and a world does not.

A closed loop has four. (1) Echo: what arrives is a function of what you did, at a fixed delay. (2) Clock: the world only moves in 40 ms frames. (3) Clean stimuli: every arrival is a single channel at a single rate, with none of the mixing a real environment has. (4) Reward from nowhere: dopamine arrives on a schedule that has nothing to do with sugar. Of these only the first is available from inside, without a ruler and a stopwatch, to a brain that can compare its own output with its input—and that comparison is a circuit real flies have: corollary discharge, the copy of a motor command that visual and mechanosensory neurons use to cancel self-generated sensation [1], [2]. This paper points that circuit at the world instead of the body, gives it a lever, and watches what happens.

The mechanism is two lines long. Let et be the brain's event vector at window t and xt the last K = 64 windows of it. A full head ŝ = Wx, trained by normalised LMS (η = 0.5), predicts the intensity arriving on each channel; a base head b tracks the channel's running mean. On each arrival, explained = 1 − |s − ŝ| / (|s − b| + 0.05) is how much better knowing your own past did, and awareness is its slow, traffic-weighted average. The gate updates as g ← g + β·surprise·(1 − explained) − α·explained (α = 0.08, β = 0.05) and drifts back open in silence at 0.0005 per window. Both pieces are saved with the brain state: the brain is literally rewriting what it lets in.

Death is PumpBrains' own rule—a brain lives on what it earns—turned into food: energy ← energy + 0.01·(feeding − cost), where the cost of living is not a free parameter but the midpoint between the connectome's feeding score right after sugar gets in (0.475) and in silence (0.095), measured on the open world C. The same rule is applied after the fact to all four runs; the connectome never sees it.
